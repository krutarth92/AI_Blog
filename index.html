Case file — how I actually build with AI

I stopped comparing AI.
I started interviewing them.

After a year of shipping real software with ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, and Gemini, I stopped asking which one is smartest — and started asking which one I'd actually hire. This is the AI team I'd put my name behind.

12 min read Field notes, not a benchmark Updated July 2026
Keep reading

I wasn't looking for another AI. I was looking for THE AI.

For a while I ran everything through one model, switching only when it let me down — a new benchmark, a friend's recommendation, whatever headline made it sound smarter than the last one. I told myself I'd eventually find the one that did everything well enough that I'd never have to think about the choice again.

That model doesn't exist. Not because these tools aren't good — they're some of the best software I've used in my career — but because "good at everything" was never really the goal. Nobody hires an engineer because they're vaguely competent at every job in the building. You hire specialists, and you put them where their specific strengths actually matter.

Somewhere around my third rewrite of the same internal tool — once with ChatGPT doing the thinking, once with Claude, once with Copilot pair-programming the whole thing — that idea stopped being a metaphor and started being how I actually work.

I stopped comparing AI. I started interviewing them.

The problem

Benchmarks don't know what Tuesday looks like

Every few weeks, a new benchmark chart makes the rounds — a model edges out another by two percentage points on some reasoning suite, and for a day it gets treated like a coronation. I used to pay close attention to these. I don't anymore, not because they're fake, but because they don't describe the parts of my job where I actually spend time.

A benchmark doesn't know that Tuesday's task is untangling a half-written API contract, that Wednesday is a client-facing whitepaper due at 5pm, or that Thursday is spent alone in a terminal, three hours deep in a bug that only shows up in production. Those days call for different kinds of thinking, and "highest average score" is a strange way to hire for any one of them.

What actually predicts whether an AI helps me is closer to what predicts whether a new hire works out: how it handles ambiguity, whether it asks the right follow-up question, whether its first draft saves me time or creates more editing work than I started with.

None of that shows up on a leaderboard. It shows up after the fortieth prompt, in the small moments where a tool either gets out of your way or gets in it. So this isn't a benchmark comparison. It's closer to reference notes — the kind you'd hand a colleague who asked, "so which one should I actually use?"

01 — Meet the candidates

Four resumes, four very different people

Here's how I'd introduce them if they walked into a hiring panel together.

AI-01 / Applicant

ChatGPT

Suggested role — Principal Architect

ChatGPT is who I loop in when a project doesn't have a shape yet. Hand it a vague idea and it'll ask good clarifying questions, sketch three ways to structure the system, and cheerfully argue with itself about tradeoffs until you've actually got a plan.

Strengths

BrainstormingArchitectureTeachingPlanningBalanced reasoning

Weaknesses

Can gold-plate a solution if you don't rein it in — left alone, it'll design a system for ten million users when you have four hundred.

Best for

Kicking off a new project, working through an ambiguous problem, explaining a concept to a junior engineer.

Ideal team position: Principal Architect — draws the whiteboard diagram everyone else builds from.

AI-02 / Applicant

Claude

Suggested role — Senior Technical Writer

Claude is who I hand things to once they actually need to be understood by another human. Documentation that reads like documentation instead of a transcript, a whitepaper with a real argument, structured markdown that doesn't need three passes of cleanup.

Strengths

DocumentationWhitepapersLong-form writingStructured thinking

Weaknesses

Code quality is good, sometimes very good — but I've seen it swing more than I'd like across a big, messy codebase.

Best for

Writing the docs you'll actually publish, drafting a whitepaper, thinking through something with real nuance and no clean answer.

Ideal team position: Senior Technical Writer — also the one you want in the room when the argument itself needs to hold up.

AI-03 / Applicant

GitHub Copilot

Suggested role — Senior Software Engineer

Copilot doesn't want to talk about your system design. It wants to be in the file, next to the cursor, finishing the line you're already writing. Half the time I'm not really "prompting" it — I'm just typing and letting it keep up.

Strengths

In-editor codingRefactoringIDE integrationDeveloper productivity

Weaknesses

Ask it to think about anything outside the IDE — strategy, writing, planning — and you'll feel it reaching for tools it wasn't built to hold.

Best for

Writing and refactoring real code, staying in flow during implementation, catching the boring bugs before you do.

Ideal team position: Senior Software Engineer — heads-down, high output, exactly where you want your strongest implementer.

AI-04 / Applicant

Gemini

Suggested role — Research Engineer

Gemini is fast, plugged into everything Google touches, and comfortable holding a huge amount of material in its head at once. When I need to pull together research from a pile of documents, it's usually the first one I open.

Strengths

Long contextFast experimentationGoogle ecosystemResearch synthesis

Weaknesses

Output can feel a little generic until you give it more explicit direction than the others need.

Best for

Digging through a stack of long documents, quick prototyping, research that touches the Google ecosystem.

Ideal team position: Research Engineer — comes back from the library with everything you need, even if you have to shape it yourself.

02 — The interview

Five rounds that told me more than any leaderboard

Not every category made the cut for a full write-up — the complete scorecard is next. These are the five rounds where the reasoning mattered enough to spell out.

Round 01 — System architecture

Winner: ChatGPT

When I'm staring at a blank page trying to figure out how the pieces of a system should talk to each other, ChatGPT is who I bring in first. It's comfortable holding several structural options in mind at once and will actually defend the ones it thinks are wrong — which is more useful than it sounds. Arguing with a bad idea is often how you find the good one.

Round 02 — Coding

Winner: Copilot

For actually writing and shipping code, Copilot wins by a wide margin, but only inside the editor. Its advantage isn't raw code quality — Claude and ChatGPT can both write clean code — it's proximity. It sees what you're doing in real time, which means less re-explaining and more just building.

Round 03 — Documentation

Winner: Claude

This one isn't close. Claude writes documentation like someone who has read a lot of good documentation. It keeps structure without forcing it, and it's the only one of the four I trust with something long enough to actually get lost in, like a full whitepaper.

Round 04 — Debugging

Winner: Copilot

Copilot spots the obvious stuff fast — the typo, the off-by-one, the missing await. When a bug is actually structural, something wrong three layers up from where it's showing symptoms, ChatGPT's willingness to reason through a whole system tends to get there first instead. But for the bug you'll hit ten times a day, Copilot wins.

Round 05 — Long context handling

Winner: Gemini

Gemini is the one I reach for when I need to throw an entire folder of documents at something and ask what's actually going on in there. It doesn't lose the thread the way the others sometimes do once a conversation gets long.

04 — Hiring decisions

Real scenarios, real picks

The matrix is useful, but hiring decisions are made on real jobs, not spreadsheets. Here's how I'd actually staff four of them.

If I needed someone to write a whitepaper...

Hired: Claude

A whitepaper lives or dies on structure and argument, not just prose quality. Claude holds a long document's shape better than the other three, which means less time reorganizing and more time actually sharpening the point.

If I needed to build production code...

Hired: Copilot

Once there's a plan, I want the fastest path from decision to working code, with the fewest context switches. That's Copilot, sitting in the editor the whole time. (ChatGPT usually writes the plan it's executing — more on that below.)

If I needed help architecting a distributed system...

Hired: ChatGPT

This is the job that rewards someone willing to hold five bad ideas in their head just to find the one good one. ChatGPT will actually push back on your first instinct, which is exactly what you want before you commit to a system design.

If I wanted help brainstorming a startup idea...

Hired: ChatGPT

Same instinct as architecture, different domain. It's comfortable being wrong out loud, which is what a real brainstorm needs. I'll usually bring Gemini in afterward to check what's already out there before getting too attached to anything.

05 — Real builds, real teams

What the full roster actually looks like on a project

Individual picks are useful, but most real work needs more than one hire. Here's the team lineup for three projects I've actually shipped.

Shipping a SaaS MVP in a weekend

  • PlanningChatGPT breaks the idea into an actual scope, picks the stack, and flags the two decisions that'll be expensive to reverse later.
  • BuildingCopilot writes and refactors the real code once the plan exists, staying in the editor the whole way.
  • WritingClaude drafts the README, the API docs, and the one-pager you'll actually send to your first users.
  • ResearchGemini pulls together what three competitors are already doing, fast, so you're not guessing.

Why this team: nobody here is doing someone else's job. That's the whole point.

Writing a technical whitepaper for a client

  • Lead writerClaude owns the structure and the argument from first draft to final pass.
  • SparringChatGPT stress-tests the argument before it goes to the client, poking holes in the weak sections.
  • ResearchGemini gathers the supporting data and anything time-sensitive that needs to be current.
  • BenchCopilot — this isn't a writing job, so it sits this one out.

Why this team: good writing needs one clear owner, not a committee. Everyone else is here to make the owner's draft better, not to write their own version.

Fixing a production bug at 2am

  • First lookCopilot is right there in the file, fastest feedback loop, catches what's obvious before you've finished your coffee.
  • EscalationChatGPT gets called in if the bug turns out to be structural — something wrong three layers up from where it's showing symptoms.
  • PostmortemClaude writes up what happened once it's fixed, while it's still fresh.
  • BenchGemini — nothing here needs a document pile or a Google integration tonight.

Why this team: half of good debugging is knowing which of these four to open first. The other half is caffeine.

The plot twist

Here's the thing I didn't expect going in: there is no perfect hire.

Every one of these four is genuinely excellent at part of the job and genuinely mediocre at the rest of it. ChatGPT will out-architect anyone in the room and then hand you documentation you'll want to rewrite. Claude will write a whitepaper you're proud to send and then need a second pass on a gnarly recursive function. Copilot will out-produce all three of them on a Tuesday afternoon and have nothing useful to say about your five-year architecture. Gemini will read a hundred documents faster than you can open them and then need more hand-holding on anything that requires a strong opinion.

None of that is a flaw to fix. It's just what specialization looks like.

The engineers I respect most didn't get that way by being equally good at everything — they got good at knowing who to ask. Turns out the same is true for AI. The upgrade isn't a smarter model. It's a better team.

Conclusion

So — which AI is the best?

Wrong question. I hope that's clear by now, if it wasn't already.

The future everyone keeps describing isn't one model that replaces you. It's a small team — some of it human, some of it not — where every member is doing the part of the job they're actually good at, and nobody's pretending to be a generalist they're not.

I stopped looking for the smartest AI a while ago. I built a team instead. Yours won't look exactly like mine — your work is different, your taste is different, and part of this list will probably change again by the time you read it. That's fine. Interview them yourself.

The future isn't replacing yourself with one AI.
The future is building the right AI team.
